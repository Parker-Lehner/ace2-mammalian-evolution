# Ecological and Lineage-Associated Molecular Evolution of ACE2 Across Mammals

Running title: Ecological ACE2 evolution across mammals

Article type: Article

Author: Parker Lehner

Corresponding author: Parker Lehner; Independent Researcher, St. James, New York, USA; lehnerparker@gmail.com; ORCID: not available

## Abstract

Angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) is a conserved mammalian membrane-associated peptidase embedded in renin-angiotensin physiology, yet broad conservation need not imply uniform molecular evolution. We analyzed a public mammalian ACE2 dataset comprising 244 curated protein records and 238 codon-level taxa to test whether ecological and lineage-associated foregrounds show episodic molecular evolution against a constrained architectural background. Site-level analyses identified 28 FUBAR candidate positive-selection sites, 95 MEME sites at p <= 0.05, and 23 FUBAR/MEME overlap sites. Two overlap residues, Q24 and D30, mapped to provisional receptor-interface annotations and are retained as structurally relevant follow-up candidates. HyPhy BUSTED detected significant episodic diversifying selection on cetacean, deep-diving, aquatic, and marine foreground branch sets, with likelihood-ratio statistics from 48.417 to 68.928 and p-values from 1.53e-11 to 5.55e-16. aBSREL prioritized Mesoplodon mirus and Leptonychotes weddellii as branch-level candidates, and reduced fixed-branch PAML analyses recovered concordant foreground signal as secondary model-family support. Together, these results support a model in which mammalian ACE2 retains conserved protein architecture while exhibiting localized ecological and lineage-associated evolutionary heterogeneity.

## Keywords

ACE2; molecular evolution; episodic diversifying selection; marine mammals; codon models; comparative genomics

## Introduction

Angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) occupies an unusual position in mammalian molecular evolution. It is a deeply conserved protein with a recognizable architecture, a defined enzymatic role, and a membrane-associated cellular context, yet it also contains residue-level features that vary across vertebrates and can influence structurally important molecular interactions. ACE2 was originally described as an ACE-related carboxypeptidase capable of processing angiotensin peptides (Donoghue et al. 2000; Tipnis et al. 2000). Subsequent physiological and structural studies established that ACE2 is not merely a sequence homolog of ACE but a functionally important component of the renin-angiotensin system, with roles in cardiovascular and pulmonary biology (Crackower et al. 2002; Imai et al. 2005). The protein therefore provides a useful evolutionary system in which conservation, constraint, and localized sequence variation can be evaluated as interacting features of the same evolutionary history.

The biological context of ACE2 creates a strong expectation of structural constraint. ACE2 is a membrane-associated peptidase whose extracellular region includes a catalytic peptidase domain and a collectrin-like region, followed by membrane-proximal and transmembrane sequence. Crystal structures of human ACE2 revealed substantial conformational movement associated with ligand binding and catalysis (Towler et al. 2004), indicating that sequence change must be evaluated in the context of a folded protein with domain-level architecture and functional surfaces. In such proteins, broad conservation does not imply evolutionary stasis. A constrained protein may retain its core catalytic and architectural framework while experiencing heterogeneous evolutionary pressures at particular residues, domains, branches, or ecological contexts. This distinction is central to interpreting ACE2: the relevant evolutionary question is not simply whether the protein is conserved, but whether conservation at the architectural scale coexists with localized molecular evolutionary heterogeneity.

Codon-level models provide a framework for testing this possibility. In protein-coding genes, the ratio and distribution of nonsynonymous and synonymous substitutions can reveal departures from a simple model of uniform purifying selection. Site-level methods such as FUBAR and MEME ask whether particular codons show evidence of pervasive or episodic diversifying selection (Murrell et al. 2012, 2013), while gene-wide and branch-level methods such as BUSTED and aBSREL test whether selection is concentrated on specified sets of branches or individual lineages (Murrell et al. 2015; Smith et al. 2015). These methods are particularly useful for constrained proteins because they can detect cases in which selection is sparse, episodic, lineage-specific, or limited to a small subset of codons.

Comparative mammalian genomics provides the taxonomic scale needed to apply these models meaningfully. Broad sampling across mammalian orders increases the opportunity to distinguish lineage-specific patterns from idiosyncratic variation in a small set of taxa. It also allows ecological foregrounds to be treated explicitly: groups of branches can be labeled according to shared ecological or evolutionary contexts, and statistical models can then ask whether those foregrounds show evidence of distinct molecular evolutionary dynamics. Such analyses remain sensitive to sampling, annotation quality, and foreground definition, but they provide a more informative test than simple pairwise comparisons or small alignments focused on a few species.

Marine and diving mammals offer a particularly useful ecological context because they represent repeated transitions into aquatic environments, accompanied by physiological changes related to oxygen storage, circulation, pressure tolerance, locomotion, and foraging ecology. Classical work on myoglobin illustrates how a protein with a direct role in oxygen storage can show repeated molecular changes associated with diving capacity (Mirceta et al. 2013). ACE2 is different from this classical diving system: it is not an oxygen-storage protein, and the present analysis does not assume that it directly mediates diving performance. Marine and diving mammals nevertheless provide a biologically meaningful set of lineages in which to ask whether a conserved membrane-associated protein shows ecological foreground-associated molecular evolutionary heterogeneity. This framing uses ecology as a hypothesis-generating context for molecular evolution while keeping adaptive causation as a question for future functional work.

Prior comparative work has emphasized both ACE2 conservation and residue-level variation. Comparative structural analyses of ACE2 across vertebrates have highlighted broad conservation of the protein while identifying differences at receptor-interface residues and other sites of potential structural interest (Damas et al. 2020). Much of this work has been motivated by receptor biology and host-range prediction, but the same comparative signal is also relevant to molecular evolution more broadly. If ACE2 combines conserved architecture with lineage-specific variation at functionally relevant surfaces, then it is an appropriate target for codon-model analyses that separate site-level, branch-level, and foreground-associated patterns.

This study addresses the gap between comparative structural surveys of ACE2 and ecological molecular evolution. Previous ACE2 comparisons have documented conservation and residue variation, but broad public-data codon analyses have not fully tested whether ecological foregrounds across mammals show concordant episodic molecular evolutionary heterogeneity. Here, we assemble and curate mammalian ACE2 orthologs, construct protein and codon alignments, map candidate sites to human ACE2 coordinates, and integrate site-level, branch-level, gene-wide foreground, reduced PAML sensitivity, and candidate-site enrichment analyses. The central hypothesis is focused: ACE2 is expected to remain broadly conserved at the architectural scale, while ecological and lineage-associated branch sets may show localized molecular evolutionary heterogeneity. The study tests for heterogeneous evolutionary dynamics without assigning a direct physiological mechanism, a diving-adaptation role, or viral-adaptation meaning to receptor-interface residues alone.

This framing also clarifies the role of statistical evidence in the manuscript. A significant codon-model result is not equivalent to a complete adaptive explanation. Rather, different model classes answer different questions about where and how molecular evolutionary rates depart from a homogeneous background. Site-level models identify residues that merit closer inspection; foreground models test whether specified ecological or lineage sets contain episodic signal; branch-level models identify individual lineages that may contribute disproportionately to that signal; and enrichment analyses ask whether candidate residues show foreground-distinguishing states more often than expected from the remainder of the protein. The strength of the present design lies in integrating complementary model classes under a consistent evidentiary framework.

Accordingly, the study is organized around three linked objectives. First, we evaluate whether a broad mammalian ACE2 dataset supports the expected pattern of domain-scale conservation and coordinate-level comparability. Second, we test whether codon-level site models and ecological foreground models reveal localized molecular evolutionary heterogeneity. Third, we integrate branch-level candidates, structural annotations, reduced PAML sensitivity results, and exploratory enrichment trends to identify hypotheses that could be followed with structural or functional work. This structure keeps the analysis focused on molecular evolutionary heterogeneity while preserving the interpretive discipline required for a single-gene public-data study.

## Results

### Public mammalian ACE2 dataset and coordinate framework

The curated dataset contained 244 mammalian ACE2 protein records derived from public NCBI/RefSeq-style sources. This sampling provided broad mammalian representation while retaining accession-level traceability for each sequence. Protein-level analyses retained the full curated set, whereas codon-level selection analyses were restricted to 238 taxa after excluding 6 records with ambiguous codons or internal stop-codon issues. The codon alignment contained 2934 nucleotide positions after back-translation from the protein alignment, and the pruned tree retained 238 taxa with no codon taxa missing from the tree (Fig. 1; Online Resource 1).

This curation outcome is important because the analysis depends on distinguishing true evolutionary signal from artifacts of sequence retrieval, isoform duplication, or low-quality coding sequence annotation. ACE2 is generally similar in length across mammals, but even small alignment or CDS errors can bias codon models toward apparent nonsynonymous change. The workflow therefore treated protein-level and codon-level analyses separately: sequences could remain informative for domain and coordinate summaries while being excluded from codon-model tests if their CDS translation was ambiguous or incompatible with the curated protein sequence.

The retained sequences showed the expected overall ACE2 length distribution for mammals, supporting the use of a shared domain and coordinate framework. The protein alignment contained 978 amino-acid columns, reflecting both the conserved core of the protein and alignment positions introduced by lineage-specific insertions or terminal differences. Sequence-level QC was used not as a post hoc exclusion mechanism for every minor gap, but as a way to identify records whose alignment behavior could compromise codon-model interpretation. This distinction is important because overly aggressive filtering can remove real evolutionary variation, whereas overly permissive filtering can allow annotation artifacts to enter selection tests. The final dataset therefore balances breadth of mammalian sampling with codon-level conservatism.

Human ACE2 coordinate mapping provided the common numbering system used throughout the analysis. Because alignment columns do not correspond one-to-one to human residue positions in the presence of indels, the coordinate map was used to translate candidate codon positions into human ACE2 residue numbers when possible. This step allowed site-level model outputs, PAML BEB candidates, receptor-interface annotations, and broad domain labels to be compared in a shared coordinate framework. The result is a traceable dataset suitable for asking whether ACE2 evolution is dominated by broad structural conservation alone or whether localized heterogeneous patterns are detectable against that conserved background.

The coordinate framework also improves interpretive control. Candidate sites reported only by alignment position can be difficult to compare with structural literature or independent ACE2 studies, especially when different alignments handle terminal and loop regions differently. Mapping to human ACE2 coordinates does not make every site functionally interpretable, but it provides a stable reference for distinguishing N-terminal, peptidase-domain, collectrin-like, and membrane-proximal contexts. Sites that could not be mapped cleanly were retained in the underlying output tables but were not elevated into structural claims.

### Site-level screens identify localized ACE2 heterogeneity

The first analytical layer asked whether individual codons show evidence of positive or diversifying selection across the mammalian ACE2 alignment. FUBAR identified 28 candidate positive-selection sites at posterior probability >= 0.90, whereas MEME identified 95 sites at p <= 0.05 and 114 sites at p <= 0.10 (Murrell et al. 2012, 2013). These counts indicate that the ACE2 alignment is not evolutionarily uniform at the codon scale. Even in a protein with conserved domain structure, a subset of positions shows patterns consistent with elevated nonsynonymous substitution relative to synonymous change.

The FUBAR and MEME results are complementary. FUBAR is most directly informative for sites showing pervasive diversifying selection across the phylogeny, whereas MEME can detect episodic selection affecting only a subset of branches. Their overlap is therefore useful as a conservative prioritization layer, although functional importance cannot be inferred from overlap alone. In this dataset, 23 sites were supported by both FUBAR and MEME under the exploratory overlap threshold. The overlap set provides a manageable group of residues for structural and lineage-focused follow-up while acknowledging that MEME-only sites may represent episodic changes not expected to appear strongly in a pervasive-selection model.

The distribution of site-level signal is consistent with localized heterogeneity, not global acceleration. A large, structurally constrained protein can produce many nominally variable positions simply because it spans hundreds of residues and many mammalian branches, but the joint FUBAR/MEME overlap provides a more focused subset than either method alone. The overlap should be understood as a ranking device: it identifies sites where two different statistical assumptions converge, not as a binary declaration that all other sites are irrelevant. This is especially important for ACE2 because functional surfaces, loop regions, and lineage-specific substitutions may evolve episodically and unevenly across the tree.

Two overlap residues, Q24 and D30 in human ACE2 coordinates, mapped to provisional receptor-interface annotations (Fig. 2; Online Resource 2). These sites are biologically interesting because they occur near an annotated interaction surface and are supported by more than one site-level screen. Their receptor-interface context prioritizes them for structural modeling, mutagenesis, or comparative receptor analysis. The site-level screen therefore connects localized ACE2 variation to plausible structural context while leaving viral adaptation, host susceptibility, and ecological mechanism as separate questions.

Other overlap sites mapped across broader ACE2 regions instead of being confined to a single obvious functional surface. This broader distribution favors a landscape-level interpretation. If candidate sites were concentrated exclusively at catalytic or receptor-interface residues, a narrower mechanistic hypothesis might be more compelling. Instead, the current pattern suggests that ACE2 heterogeneity may be distributed across multiple structural contexts, with some residues more immediately interpretable than others. The manuscript therefore emphasizes a landscape of candidate residues rather than a single decisive site.

### Ecological foregrounds show episodic molecular evolutionary heterogeneity

The strongest statistical evidence in the analysis came from BUSTED foreground tests. BUSTED detected significant episodic diversifying selection on each tested ecological foreground branch set: cetacean LRT = 48.417, p = 1.53e-11; deep diving LRT = 68.928, p = 5.55e-16; aquatic LRT = 65.846, p = 2.50e-15; and marine LRT = 65.659, p = 2.78e-15 (Fig. 3; Online Resource 3). These results support episodic diversifying selection affecting foreground branch sets, contrasting with a model in which ACE2 evolves under uniform constraint across all mammalian lineages.

The foreground categories are biologically related and partly overlapping. Many cetaceans are marine and deep-diving; the aquatic and marine categories also share numerous taxa. For that reason, the four significant BUSTED results are best read as concordant evidence from related ecological codings, not independent confirmations of a single adaptive process. Together, they indicate that ACE2 contains a foreground-associated component of episodic molecular evolutionary heterogeneity in lineages associated with aquatic or marine ecological histories. The most conservative reading is therefore statistical and evolutionary: ACE2 evolution is heterogeneous across mammalian branches, and that heterogeneity is detectable when foreground branches are defined by broad ecological labels.

This result matters because it shifts the interpretation of ACE2 away from a simple conservation-only model. The dataset does not indicate wholesale ACE2 remodeling in marine or diving mammals, but BUSTED does indicate that the distribution of nonsynonymous change is not fully explained by background mammalian constraint alone. This pattern is consistent with a protein that remains structurally constrained while experiencing episodic shifts in selective regime on particular branch sets. Such a result is compatible with ecological or lineage-associated hypotheses, but it does not by itself identify the selective agent, physiological consequence, or functional effect of individual substitutions.

The concordance between foreground definitions is nevertheless informative. Cetacean, aquatic, marine, and deep-diving labels are not interchangeable, but their overlap means that a shared signal across them points toward a general aquatic/marine-associated region of the mammalian tree rather than an arbitrary coding artifact. The strongest inference is therefore at the level of evolutionary heterogeneity: ACE2 contains branch-set-associated signal that becomes visible when lineages with aquatic or marine ecological histories are modeled as foregrounds. The analysis allows heterogeneity within foregrounds and does not require the same residues to have changed in every foreground lineage.

The magnitude of the BUSTED likelihood-ratio statistics also supports treating these foregrounds as central results. The tests returned small p-values across all four foreground configurations, and the signal was not restricted to a single narrow label. Statistical strength narrows the evolutionary interpretation but does not identify ecological causation. BUSTED tests whether episodic diversifying selection occurred on foreground branches at at least one site; ecological mechanism remains a biological hypothesis for future work.

### Branch-level candidates and sensitivity analyses prioritize follow-up

Branch-level aBSREL analyses refined the foreground signal by identifying candidate lineages within the broader ecological tests. Mesoplodon mirus was detected across aquatic, cetacean, deep-diving, and marine foreground configurations, with LRT values near 167 in parsed outputs. Leptonychotes weddellii was detected in aquatic, deep-diving, and marine contexts, with corrected p-values near 0.01 and LRT values near 15 (Fig. 4; Online Resource 3). The recurrence of Mesoplodon mirus across foreground configurations suggests that this branch contributes strongly to the foreground-associated signal, whereas the repeated detection of Leptonychotes weddellii identifies an additional marine/diving mammal lineage for follow-up.

These branch-level candidates are prioritization results. aBSREL is useful for locating branches with evidence of episodic diversifying selection, but a significant branch does not automatically identify a phenotype, ecological mechanism, or functionally validated residue. In practical terms, these results identify lineages in which sequence quality, alignment placement, branch length, and residue changes deserve close inspection before stronger biological claims are made. They also provide a bridge between gene-wide foreground tests and residue-level analyses: BUSTED indicates that foreground branch sets contain episodic selection signal, while aBSREL helps identify branches that may contribute disproportionately to that signal.

Reduced fixed-branch PAML branch-site analyses provided a secondary model-family check. The reduced PAML tests supported the same major foreground categories: cetacean LRT = 31.904, p = 1.62e-08; deep diving LRT = 31.126, p = 2.42e-08; aquatic LRT = 22.213, p = 2.44e-06; and marine LRT = 22.213, p = 2.44e-06 (Fig. 3; Online Resource 4). Because these PAML analyses used reduced fixed-branch datasets, they are secondary to the full HyPhy foreground tests. Their value is that they point in the same direction as the BUSTED foreground tests, suggesting that the foreground signal is not limited to a single model family.

Candidate-site enrichment analyses added an exploratory residue-state layer. These tests asked whether FUBAR/MEME overlap sites were enriched for foreground-distinguishing residue states relative to noncandidate ACE2 sites. The strongest trend was observed for cetaceans, with odds ratio = 4.561, raw Fisher p = 1.29e-02, and BH-FDR = 0.0773. Aquatic, marine, and deep-diving foregrounds showed weaker trends with BH-FDR values above the usual 0.10 exploratory threshold, and pinniped and semi-aquatic categories did not provide strong enrichment evidence. These results are useful for prioritizing residues and foregrounds, while adaptation requires stronger functional and comparative support. Considered together, the Results support a layered interpretation: ACE2 is conserved in architecture, heterogeneous at selected sites, foreground-associated in gene-wide tests, and branch-prioritized in a small number of lineages.

The layered structure of the evidence is important for evaluating robustness. Site-level analyses alone would show that ACE2 contains candidate residues but would not connect them to ecological foregrounds. BUSTED alone would support foreground-associated episodic selection but would not identify candidate branches or residues. aBSREL narrows attention to particular lineages, while PAML sensitivity analyses provide a second model-family check on the foreground pattern. Enrichment tests are weaker and more exploratory, but they help ask whether candidate sites carry foreground-distinguishing residue states. The combined result is a coherent comparative-genomic pattern that justifies a detailed discussion of ACE2 heterogeneity under constraint.

## Discussion

The expanded analysis supports a model in which ACE2 evolution is shaped by both constraint and localized heterogeneity. This combination is not paradoxical. Conserved proteins often retain their domain architecture, catalytic framework, and broad functional context over long evolutionary timescales while still permitting substitutions at particular residues or along particular branches. For ACE2, this means that conservation of the peptidase and membrane-associated architecture can coexist with episodic diversifying selection at a subset of codons and lineages. The present results are therefore more consistent with heterogeneous evolutionary dynamics under constraint than with either a strict conservation-only model or a model of wholesale protein remodeling.

The ecological foreground results are the strongest basis for this interpretation. BUSTED detected significant episodic diversifying selection across cetacean, deep-diving, aquatic, and marine foreground definitions, and reduced PAML analyses supported the same general foreground categories as a secondary check. Because these ecological labels overlap substantially, they are treated as related encodings of an aquatic/marine evolutionary context, not independent biological replicates. The repeated foreground signal nevertheless indicates that ACE2 evolution is not distributed uniformly across the sampled mammalian tree. This pattern raises a biologically plausible hypothesis: some aquatic or marine-associated lineages may have experienced shifts in ACE2 selective regime, even though the specific selective pressures remain unresolved.

Marine mammals are useful in this context because they represent repeated ecological transitions accompanied by profound physiological and life-history changes. However, the comparison with myoglobin is instructive precisely because ACE2 is different. Myoglobin is directly connected to oxygen storage and has a clear mechanistic relationship to diving capacity (Mirceta et al. 2013). ACE2, by contrast, is a membrane-associated peptidase embedded in renin-angiotensin biology and structural interaction surfaces. The present data therefore do not assign ACE2 a direct role in diving performance. Marine and diving mammals provide a repeated ecological framework in which to test whether a constrained protein shows lineage-associated molecular evolutionary heterogeneity. That distinction preserves the biological interest of the result without extending it beyond what the data can support.

The residue-level findings sharpen this interpretation but do not resolve mechanism. Q24 and D30 are notable because they occur in human ACE2 coordinates with provisional receptor-interface annotations and appear in the FUBAR/MEME overlap set. These sites are sensible candidates for structural follow-up because they connect codon-model signal to an annotated molecular surface. Receptor-interface context alone is insufficient to infer viral adaptation or host-susceptibility evolution. Comparative structural analyses have shown that ACE2 interface residues vary across vertebrates and can be relevant to receptor biology (Damas et al. 2020), but establishing functional consequences requires independent structural modeling, binding assays, or experimental validation. Q24, D30, and other candidate residues are therefore prioritized hypotheses, not resolved adaptive substitutions.

The branch-level findings also require careful interpretation. Mesoplodon mirus and Leptonychotes weddellii emerged as aBSREL candidates, suggesting that specific branches may contribute strongly to the foreground-associated signal. This is valuable because it connects group-level signal to specific lineages. At the same time, branch-level episodic selection tests can be sensitive to alignment quality, branch length, taxon sampling, and annotation error. A branch candidate should trigger targeted inspection of sequence provenance, CDS translation, local alignment structure, residue-state changes, and phylogenetic placement before lineage-specific biological claims are advanced. In this sense, aBSREL provides a rational path for future validation.

Several limitations define the scope of the study. First, ACE2 is a single gene, and single-gene analyses cannot establish pathway-level adaptation. Renin-angiotensin, hypoxia, vascular, renal, and pulmonary traits involve interacting networks of genes, regulatory elements, and physiological systems. Second, the ecological labels used here are simplified binary or categorical foregrounds. Aquatic, marine, cetacean, and deep-diving categories capture broad evolutionary contexts but do not encode quantitative dive depth, dive duration, foraging ecology, body size, or habitat use with the resolution needed for trait-gradient inference. Third, the analyses rely on public sequence annotations and orthology assignments, which are powerful but not error-free. Fourth, the reduced fixed-branch PAML analyses are useful as secondary model-family support but do not replace full free-branch validation in all taxa. Finally, no functional assays were performed, so molecular evolutionary patterns remain hypotheses about selection, not demonstrations of biochemical or physiological effect.

The phylogenetic structure of the data imposes an additional interpretive constraint. Marine and diving mammals are not randomly distributed across the mammalian tree, and several foreground definitions share the same branches. As a result, the analyses cannot separate every possible ecological, historical, and lineage-specific explanation. For example, a cetacean-associated signal may reflect ecological pressures, lineage history, taxon sampling, or a combination of these factors. The manuscript therefore uses the phrase ecological and lineage-associated deliberately: the statistical signal is associated with foreground branch definitions, but disentangling ecological causality from phylogenetic history requires additional data and models.

Another limitation concerns functional annotation. Human ACE2 coordinates provide an essential reference, but annotations derived from human structural context do not automatically transfer functional meaning to every mammalian substitution. A residue near an interface in human ACE2 may have different local context in another species because of neighboring substitutions, conformational differences, glycosylation, or broader domain interactions. This does not undermine the value of structural mapping, but it argues against treating mapped residues as mechanistic endpoints. Structural annotation should be viewed as a bridge from statistical signal to testable hypothesis.

Future work should move in three directions. The first is structural: candidate residues should be mapped onto experimentally resolved or high-confidence ACE2 structures, evaluated for solvent exposure and domain context, and compared across foreground and background lineages. The second is functional: substitutions prioritized by overlapping site-level, branch-level, and structural evidence could be evaluated using binding, stability, catalytic, or expression assays descheduled on the biological hypothesis. The third is comparative: ACE2 should be analyzed alongside additional renin-angiotensin, vascular, hypoxia, and marine-mammal candidate genes to determine whether ACE2 is part of a broader pathway-level pattern or an isolated single-gene signal. Such extensions would allow stronger ecological-genomic inference than ACE2 alone can support.

The most direct next step is to strengthen the bridge between statistical and biological interpretation. Full-dataset branch-site validation, more detailed ecological trait coding, and explicit comparison with species-tree-based models would help test whether the foreground signal persists under alternative assumptions. Quantitative traits such as maximum dive depth, dive duration, foraging depth, and body mass could also be incorporated where reliable data are available, but missing or uneven trait coverage should be handled transparently rather than imputed into artificial precision. A broader pathway analysis could then ask whether ACE2 is exceptional or whether similar foreground-associated heterogeneity occurs across interacting physiological systems.

The broader evolutionary takeaway is that structural constraint and ecological heterogeneity need not be opposing explanations. ACE2 appears to retain a conserved mammalian architecture while exhibiting localized residue- and lineage-associated evolutionary shifts. The strongest current evidence supports foreground-associated episodic molecular evolutionary heterogeneity, especially in aquatic/marine-associated branch definitions, with branch and residue candidates that can guide future work. This conclusion is intentionally restrained but biologically meaningful: ACE2 is not shown here to be a diving-adaptation gene, yet its evolutionary history across mammals is richer than a simple model of uniform conservation.

## Materials and Methods

### Sequence retrieval and dataset curation

The analysis used public mammalian ACE2 records from NCBI/RefSeq-style sources as the primary sequence source. Retrieval was organized around ACE2 orthology rather than keyword-only inclusion. The curated metadata table retained accession identifiers, scientific names, sequence lengths, source database labels, original FASTA headers, and retrieval timestamps. Human ACE2 was used as the coordinate reference for residue mapping, and mammalian records were retained only when they were compatible with an ACE2 ortholog interpretation. UniProt fallback records were kept separate from the primary RefSeq-style set unless they had been manually reviewed; ambiguous fallback records were not merged into the main codon-analysis dataset.

Initial filtering removed non-mammalian entries, obvious fragments, duplicate isoforms where a primary representative could be selected, likely pseudogenes, and records whose annotation or length made them incompatible with a typical mammalian ACE2 ortholog. For species with multiple isoforms or predicted records, the workflow prioritized a single representative ACE2 sequence per species to reduce pseudoreplication. The final protein dataset contained 244 curated mammalian ACE2 records. The coding-sequence retrieval step then attempted to pair each retained protein record with a compatible CDS. CDS records were translated and compared with the corresponding protein sequence; records with internal stop codons, ambiguous codons, or translation mismatch problems were excluded from codon-level selection tests. This produced 238 codon-level taxa and excluded 6 records from codon analyses.

The separation between protein-level and codon-level retention was intentional. A protein sequence with useful domain information may still be unsuitable for dN/dS-based analyses if the corresponding CDS contains ambiguity or stop-codon artifacts. Conversely, codon models are particularly sensitive to frame, translation, and alignment errors. For this reason, the manuscript reports protein and codon dataset sizes separately and treats the 238-taxon codon alignment as the authoritative input for selection analyses.

Taxonomic validation was performed through the retained scientific names and sequence identifiers in the accession metadata. Sequence identifiers were standardized to combine species names with accession identifiers, reducing ambiguity when matching alignments, trees, and result tables. Records whose taxonomic placement or annotation suggested non-mammalian contamination, paralogy, or fragmentary retrieval were excluded during curation before model fitting. The retained metadata therefore provide a traceable path from each analyzed sequence back to its source record and retrieval date.

Duplicate and isoform handling followed a conservative one-representative-per-species strategy unless a biologically justified duplication had been documented. This was important because codon models assume that the tips being compared represent homologous sequences evolving along the specified tree. Including multiple isoforms from the same species without a clear rationale could overweight particular taxa and introduce transcript-annotation differences that are not evolutionary substitutions between species. When isoform labels were present in headers, the workflow prioritized records that best represented full-length ACE2 orthologs and preserved header information for audit.

### Alignment, codon preparation, and coordinate mapping

Protein sequences were aligned using MAFFT, which is appropriate for large multiple-sequence alignments and provides reproducible handling of indel-rich regions when configured consistently (Katoh and Standley 2013). The curated protein alignment had an alignment length of 978 amino-acid columns. Alignment QC summarized ungapped length, gap fraction, missing fraction, and sequence-level flags. The QC screen retained 240 protein records as pass-level entries and flagged 4 gap-heavy or otherwise concerning records for cautionary review. These QC summaries were used to identify potential outliers before downstream interpretation.

Codon alignment was generated by back-translating the protein alignment using the matched CDS records. This approach preserves the protein-level homology structure while producing codon triplets suitable for selection analyses. The resulting codon alignment length was 2934 nucleotides and was verified to be divisible by three. Translation checks confirmed that retained CDS records did not contain internal stop codons and matched the expected protein sequence after alignment-aware processing. Taxa failing these checks were excluded from codon-level tests but remained documented in the codon-alignment status table.

Gap handling was performed at the sequence and alignment-QC level without indiscriminately deleting all gapped columns. This is appropriate for a broad mammalian alignment in which indels may occur in terminal or loop regions while the core protein remains alignable. Sequence-level gap fractions and missing fractions were used to flag records requiring caution, and codon-level tests were restricted to sequences with reliable CDS mappings. This approach preserves informative homologous positions while reducing the risk that poor-quality sequence records drive selection results.

Human ACE2 coordinate mapping was used to connect alignment columns to interpretable residue numbers. Because insertions and deletions can shift alignment positions relative to human ACE2, each alignment column was mapped to a human residue coordinate when possible. Candidate sites from FUBAR, MEME, PAML BEB outputs, and structural/domain annotations were then integrated through this coordinate map. This step allowed statistical outputs to be reported in a biologically interpretable coordinate system while preserving the underlying alignment-site traceability.

The coordinate map also served as a guardrail for manuscript reporting. Sites were described using human ACE2 residue numbers only when the alignment-to-human mapping supported that assignment. Sites without confident mapping remained available in supplemental alignment-position outputs but were not elevated into residue-specific narrative claims. This distinction helps keep the manuscript reproducible and prevents apparent precision from exceeding the coordinate evidence.

### Phylogenetic framework

An ACE2 maximum-likelihood gene tree was inferred with IQ-TREE as a quality-control tree for orthology and taxon sanity checking (Minh et al. 2020). The tree was used to identify gross phylogenetic inconsistencies, non-mammalian contamination, and mismatches between the sequence alignment and downstream codon-analysis inputs. The codon-pruned tree contained 238 taxa after removing 6 records that lacked selection-ready codon data; no codon taxa were missing from the pruned tree. This ensured that tree and alignment taxon sets matched for codon-model analyses.

The inferred ACE2 tree was treated primarily as an analysis and QC framework, not as an independent estimate of mammalian species history. This distinction matters because a single gene tree may contain gene-specific signal, alignment artifacts, or branch-length patterns that differ from the species tree. For the purposes of the present codon-model analyses, the key requirement was that the tree and alignment were congruent, mammalian, and free of obvious paralog or contamination signals. The tree also provided the branch structure on which ecological foreground labels were applied.

Foreground labels were applied to tree branches according to the project ecological metadata. The labels used in the primary foreground tests were cetacean, aquatic, marine, and deep-diving. Because these labels are related and overlapping, they were analyzed as alternative codings of ecological/lineage context, not statistically independent experiments. This designation was carried through the Results and Discussion to prevent overinterpretation of concordant foreground significance as independent replication.

### Site-level selection analyses

Site-level codon analyses used FUBAR and MEME from the HyPhy model framework. FUBAR was used to identify sites with evidence of pervasive diversifying selection across the phylogeny, summarized here at posterior probability >= 0.90 (Murrell et al. 2013). MEME was used to detect episodic diversifying selection at individual sites, including cases where selection affects only a subset of branches (Murrell et al. 2012). MEME sites were summarized at p <= 0.05 for the main screen and p <= 0.10 for exploratory overlap with FUBAR.

The use of both methods reflects their different assumptions. FUBAR is efficient and useful for identifying sites with broad evidence of positive selection, whereas MEME is more sensitive to episodic patterns that may be concentrated in particular lineages. Overlap between FUBAR and MEME was therefore treated as a prioritization criterion, not a requirement for biological relevance. Sites supported by both methods were mapped to human ACE2 coordinates and compared with provisional domain and receptor-interface annotations. Site-level outputs were not interpreted as functional proof; they were used to identify residues where statistical signal and structural context intersect.

Posterior probabilities and p-values were not combined into a single composite score. Instead, the manuscript reports model-specific evidence classes and uses overlap qualitatively. This avoids implying that FUBAR and MEME measure the same quantity or that a simple arithmetic combination would produce a more valid biological ranking. The site-level table remains the authoritative source for individual residue support, while the main text focuses on broad counts and selected structurally interpretable examples.

### Foreground and branch-level selection analyses

BUSTED was used as the primary gene-wide foreground-selection test (Murrell et al. 2015). Foregrounds were defined for cetacean, deep-diving, aquatic, and marine branch sets based on the project ecological metadata. These categories were selected because they represent related aquatic/marine ecological contexts and because prior marine-mammal molecular evolution work provides a clear comparative motivation without implying that ACE2 itself is a classical diving protein (Mirceta et al. 2013). BUSTED tests whether at least one site on the specified foreground branches has experienced episodic diversifying selection, making it well suited for detecting sparse foreground-associated signal in a constrained gene.

The ecological foregrounds were not treated as independent biological replicates. Cetacean, marine, aquatic, and deep-diving labels overlap substantially, and several taxa contribute to more than one foreground definition. The foreground analyses therefore ask whether related ecological codings recover concordant evidence of ACE2 heterogeneity, not whether four independent mechanisms have been demonstrated. This interpretive boundary is central to the manuscript.

aBSREL was used to identify individual branches showing evidence of episodic diversifying selection (Smith et al. 2015). Corrected p-values and LRT values were summarized for significant branch candidates. Branches detected by aBSREL were treated as follow-up priorities because branch-level tests can be influenced by sequence quality, branch length, local alignment behavior, and taxon sampling. The analysis therefore reports Mesoplodon mirus and Leptonychotes weddellii as candidate lineages, not resolved examples of functional adaptation.

For each foreground configuration, branch-level outputs were parsed into standardized tables containing foreground group, branch identifier, corrected p-value, uncorrected p-value, LRT, and interpretation field. This parsing step was necessary because downstream manuscript claims rely on comparing candidates across foreground definitions. Branches recurring across multiple related foregrounds were emphasized as robust prioritization candidates, whereas single-context branch detections would have been treated more cautiously.

### PAML sensitivity analyses and enrichment tests

Reduced fixed-branch PAML branch-site tests were used as a secondary model-family layer. PAML branch-site models test whether foreground branches contain a class of sites with elevated nonsynonymous substitution relative to background branches (Yang 1998). In this project, reduced foreground-specific datasets were used to improve tractability and provide a model-family comparison with the HyPhy analyses. The same major foreground categories were tested, and LRT values were evaluated using a chi-square framework with df = 1. Because these runs used a reduced fixed-branch framework, they were interpreted as supporting model-family evidence and not as a replacement for full free-branch validation.

Candidate-site enrichment analyses asked whether FUBAR/MEME overlap sites were more likely than noncandidate ACE2 sites to show foreground-distinguishing residue states. Fisher exact tests were used for foreground/background comparisons, and Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery correction was applied across tested foregrounds (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995). These analyses were explicitly exploratory. Enrichment can prioritize a foreground or residue set for follow-up, while residue-state differences may also reflect phylogenetic structure, sampling, or lineage history.

### Reproducibility and evidence traceability

The analysis package includes curated metadata, raw public retrieval outputs, processed coordinate maps, alignment QC tables, codon-alignment status files, selection summaries, foreground-test outputs, figures, and manuscript-generation scripts. The software environment is specified in `environment.yml` and includes Python, Biopython, pandas, MAFFT, IQ-TREE, HyPhy, PAML, R, and supporting comparative-phylogenetic packages. Evidence traceability was maintained by mapping each numerical manuscript claim to a source artifact in `evidence_traceability.tsv`.

The repository has been organized for GitHub release under `ace2-mammalian-evolution` and Zenodo archival before publication. This reproducibility structure is intended to allow reviewers to audit the manuscript from reported values back to specific tables and scripts.

Manuscript conclusions are tied to existing project artifacts or to the verified literature cited in the reference list. Numerical values in the Results were taken from local tables, creating a practical audit trail for scientific review and journal revision.
